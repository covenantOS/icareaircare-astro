<h2>What an AC tune-up costs in Tampa Bay</h2>

<p>Published national data for 2026 puts a basic AC tune-up between <strong>$75 and $200</strong>, with an average near $100 (<a href="https://www.angi.com/articles/ac-service-cost.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">Angi</a>, <a href="https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/heating-and-cooling/service-maintain-ac-unit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">HomeAdvisor</a>). Comprehensive maintenance, the kind that includes coil cleaning and full electrical testing rather than a filter swap and a gauge reading, runs <strong>$175 to $350</strong>, averaging around $250.</p>

<p>Annual plans sit in a different bracket. Service agreements run <strong>$150 to $500 per year</strong>, with most basic plans covering two visits landing near $250 and premium tiers with priority scheduling and parts discounts reaching $350 to $500.</p>

<p>Those are national figures, and they are the honest starting point rather than a quote. Tampa Bay skews toward the upper half of every one of those ranges, for a reason worth understanding before you shop on price.</p>

<p>Florida runs more than 2,200 cooling hours a year. A system in Wesley Chapel does roughly double the annual work of the same system in Ohio, and it does that work in salt air, afternoon thunderstorms, and pollen. The maintenance interval that keeps a northern system healthy is simply not enough here, and a technician who is thorough about it needs more time on site. You are paying for the hour, not for the checklist.</p>

<h2>What actually happens in a 90-minute tune-up</h2>

<p>Here is the honest version of a full maintenance visit, in the order it happens. If you are comparing quotes, compare against this rather than against a bullet list on a coupon.</p>

<h3>The first ten minutes: the conversation</h3>

<p>A good technician asks before touching anything. Which rooms never get comfortable. Whether the system short-cycles in the afternoon. What the electric bill did last August compared to the one before. Whether you have heard anything new, and when.</p>

<p>This is not small talk. Half the diagnostic value in a maintenance visit comes from the homeowner, because you have been living with the symptom for months and the technician has ninety minutes. A tech who skips straight to the condenser is going to miss whatever you would have told them.</p>

<h3>Minutes ten to forty: the indoor unit</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Evaporator coil.</strong> Inspected for dirt, biological growth, and corrosion. In coastal and near-coastal Florida homes, formicary corrosion puts pinhole leaks in aluminum coils faster than inland systems, and it is invisible until it is not.</li>
  <li><strong>Blower wheel and motor.</strong> Cleaned if fouled, amp draw measured against the nameplate rating. A blower pulling above spec is telling you something is dragging.</li>
  <li><strong>Drain line and pan.</strong> Cleared, flushed, and tested. Florida humidity means a working system pulls gallons of water out of your air every day, and a clogged line puts that water in your ceiling.</li>
  <li><strong>Filter.</strong> Replaced, and the tech should tell you the actual MERV rating and whether your system can breathe through it. A too-restrictive filter costs more in airflow than it buys in filtration.</li>
  <li><strong>Static pressure.</strong> Measured across the air handler. This is the reading almost nobody takes and almost everybody needs, because high static pressure is the quiet cause behind burned-out blower motors and rooms that never cool.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Minutes forty to sixty: outside at the condenser</h3>

<ul>
  <li>Condenser coil washed, and the fins straightened where a weed trimmer or hail got to them</li>
  <li>Refrigerant pressures read against ambient temperature, with superheat and subcooling calculated rather than eyeballed</li>
  <li>Compressor and fan motor amp draw measured</li>
  <li>Contactor inspected for pitting and burn, which is the single most common failure we replace in Pasco County. If yours is scorched, read our walkthrough on <a href="/blogs/ac-contactor-failure-signs/">AC contactor failure signs</a> before it strands you</li>
  <li>Capacitor tested under load, with the measured microfarad reading compared to the rated one</li>
  <li>Disconnect box opened and checked for corrosion and water intrusion</li>
</ul>

<h3>Minutes sixty to eighty: electrical, thermostat, ducts</h3>

<p>Every accessible connection torqued and checked for heat discoloration. Thermostat calibration verified against an independent thermometer. Accessible ductwork inspected for disconnected boots, crushed flex, and insulation that has given up. Temperature split measured across the coil, which tells you in one number whether the system is doing the work it claims to be doing.</p>

<h3>The last ten minutes: the report</h3>

<p>You should end the visit holding numbers. Static pressure, temperature split, amp draws, capacitor readings, refrigerant pressures, and photographs of anything that needs attention. Not a checkmark next to the word "checked."</p>

<blockquote><strong>Tim's tip:</strong> ask for the capacitor reading in microfarads and compare it to the number printed on the side of the part. A capacitor rated at 45 that measures 38 is still running your compressor today and will not be running it in August. That one number, written down twice a year, is the cheapest failure prediction in this trade.</blockquote>

<h2>The $49 tune-up problem</h2>

<p>You will see them all over Tampa Bay every spring. Sometimes $39. The math does not work, and it is worth being clear about why, because the offer itself is not the problem. What happens after it is.</p>

<p>A licensed technician in a stocked van costs a contractor real money per hour. Nobody sends one to your house for $49 and ninety minutes of labor and comes out ahead. So the visit is not the product. The visit is the lead, and it is priced to get someone through your door. What follows is usually a twenty-minute look and a recommendation that costs considerably more than $49.</p>

<p>Signs you are in one of these:</p>

<ul>
  <li>The tech is done in under half an hour</li>
  <li>No gauges came out, or they came out and no readings were written down</li>
  <li>You are shown a photo of a dirty coil and quoted a four-figure repair in the same breath</li>
  <li>The recommendation is replacement, on a system under ten years old, without a load calculation</li>
  <li>Pricing is verbal only</li>
</ul>

<p>None of that means every discounted tune-up is a trap. It means the number to ask about is not the price. It is the time on site and whether you get readings in writing. A contractor who is comfortable answering both is not the one you need to worry about.</p>

<h2>When a maintenance plan actually pays</h2>

<p>Plans get oversold, so here is the arithmetic without the pitch. A plan makes sense when it covers two visits a year, because that is the interval a Florida system needs and buying two comprehensive visits separately costs more than most annual plans do. At one visit a year the discount rarely covers the commitment.</p>

<p>What matters more than the discount is what sits underneath it:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>How is priority scheduling defined?</strong> "Priority" is meaningless unless someone will say what it means in hours during the first week of July, when every contractor in the county is backed up</li>
  <li><strong>What is the repair discount, and does it apply to parts, labor, or both?</strong> Fifteen percent off labor is a different product from fifteen percent off the invoice</li>
  <li><strong>Does the plan auto-renew, and what does cancelling cost?</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Are the two visits actually comprehensive, or is the second one a filter change?</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Does the agreement transfer if you sell the house?</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>If a contractor cannot answer those five in plain language, the plan is a subscription rather than a service. <h2>When to book it in Florida</h2>

<p>March and April. Not June.</p>

<p>The reason is capacity, not weather. Emergency call volume in this trade climbs sharply during the first sustained heat of the season, and a large share of the year's emergency work lands inside a handful of summer weeks. A tune-up booked in June competes for a slot against a house with no cooling at all, and it should lose that competition. Booked in March, you get an unhurried ninety minutes and the failing capacitor gets caught in a scheduled visit rather than on the hottest Saturday of the year.</p>

<p>If your system is already struggling, a tune-up is the wrong call. Read <a href="/blogs/why-is-my-ac-not-cooling/">why your AC is not cooling</a> first, or book <a href="/services/ac-repair-tampa/">AC repair in Tampa</a> and let a diagnostic sort it out. Maintenance is for healthy systems. Putting a maintenance visit on a broken one wastes your money and the technician's afternoon.</p>
