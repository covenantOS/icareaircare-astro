<h2>AC unit replacement cost by system size (2 to 5 ton)</h2>
<p>The single biggest driver of price is capacity, measured in tons. One ton equals 12,000 BTUs of cooling per hour. A typical Tampa Bay home needs roughly one ton per 500 to 600 square feet, but that ratio is a starting point, not a rule, because Florida's humidity, ceiling height, sun exposure, and insulation all move the target. Here is what a full single-stage 15.2 SEER2 changeout runs by size across the Tampa Bay market, drawn from <a href="https://www.angi.com/articles/how-much-does-installing-new-ac-cost/fl/tampa" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">Angi's Tampa cost data</a> and cross-checked against <a href="https://www.thisoldhouse.com/heating-cooling/3-ton-air-conditioner-cost" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">This Old House</a>. Tampa sits below the national average on replacement, because there is no heating load to design around, install volume is high, and competition is dense:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>2 ton (roughly 1,000 to 1,200 sq ft):</strong> $6,500 to $9,000 installed. Common for smaller Land O' Lakes townhomes and Zephyrhills patio homes.</li>
<li><strong>2.5 ton (roughly 1,200 to 1,500 sq ft):</strong> $7,000 to $9,500 installed.</li>
<li><strong>3 ton (roughly 1,500 to 2,000 sq ft):</strong> $7,500 to $10,500 installed. The most common size replaced in Wesley Chapel and New Tampa.</li>
<li><strong>4 ton (roughly 2,000 to 2,600 sq ft):</strong> $8,500 to $12,000 installed.</li>
<li><strong>5 ton (roughly 2,600 to 3,200 sq ft):</strong> $10,000 to $14,000 installed for larger Lutz and Odessa homes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Notice the jump from 2 ton to 5 ton is real but not enormous on the baseline tier, roughly $3,500 to $4,500. That is because a large share of the cost is labor, permitting, and the fixed work of a proper changeout, which does not scale linearly with tonnage. The equipment itself gets more expensive with size, but the crew still has to pull the old system, set the new one, braze the line set, pull a vacuum, and commission the system regardless of whether it is a 2 ton or a 5 ton.</p>

<h2>HVAC replacement cost by SEER2 efficiency tier</h2>
<p>The second big lever is efficiency. Florida sits in the DOE Southeast region, so every new split system installed here has to be at least <strong>15.2 SEER2</strong> by code. You cannot legally install a 14 SEER2 unit as a replacement in Pasco, Hillsborough, or Polk county. Above that floor, you choose how efficient and how comfortable you want to go. For a 3-ton system, which is the most common Tampa Bay changeout, the published tiers look like this:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>15.2 SEER2 single-stage (code minimum):</strong> $7,500 to $10,500. Cheapest compliant install, on/off compressor, meets code, fine for rentals and low-occupancy homes.</li>
<li><strong>16 SEER2 two-stage:</strong> $9,000 to $12,000. Roughly $1,500 over baseline for a noticeable comfort and humidity improvement.</li>
<li><strong>18 SEER2 variable-speed:</strong> $11,000 to $14,500. Entry variable-speed, the humidity-control sweet spot for owner-occupied Florida homes.</li>
<li><strong>20 to 22 SEER2 premium variable-speed:</strong> $14,000 to $18,000. Quietest, best dehumidification, highest lifetime efficiency.</li>
</ul>
<p>Read those as a stack. Every step up buys you a little more efficiency and a lot more comfort control, but the operating-cost savings alone rarely pay back the premium in Tampa Bay's runtime profile. We walk through that payback math in detail in our <a href="/blogs/seer2-explained-florida-2026/">SEER2 ratings explained for Florida</a> guide, and the short version is: buy variable-speed for humidity and quiet, not because a bigger SEER2 number will pay for itself on your TECO bill. The one exception that changes the math is a heat pump plus the federal tax credit, which we cover further down.</p>

<h2>What actually drives your AC replacement cost</h2>
<p>Two identical 3-ton 16 SEER2 quotes can legitimately differ by $3,000, and it is not always because one contractor is gouging you. Here is where the money actually goes.</p>
<h3>Equipment versus labor</h3>
<p>On a typical Tampa Bay changeout, roughly 45 to 55 percent of the price is equipment (condenser, coil, air handler) and the rest is labor, materials, permit, and overhead. Angi 2026 pegs the national labor cost to install an AC system in the $1,200 to $2,500 range on a straightforward swap, and more when ductwork or electrical work is involved. Labor is not where you want to cut, because a perfect $6,000 piece of equipment installed badly will underperform and fail early. Charge accuracy, vacuum depth, and electrical tightness matter more than the brand on the cabinet.</p>
<h3>Ductwork condition</h3>
<p>If your ducts are 20 years old, leaking at the boots, crushed in the attic, or badly sized, a new high-efficiency system cannot deliver its rated performance. Sealing or partial replacement of ductwork can add $1,500 to $4,000 depending on scope (HomeAdvisor 2026). We do not automatically upsell duct replacement, but we will tell you honestly if leaky returns are the reason your old system never kept up.</p>
<h3>The air handler and coil</h3>
<p>A true replacement includes a matched indoor unit, not just the outdoor condenser. Swapping only the condenser and leaving an old, mismatched coil is one of the most common corner-cutting moves, and it tanks the efficiency you paid for. A standalone evaporator coil replacement runs <strong>$1,400 to $2,600</strong> as an industry range (Angi 2026), which is exactly why "condenser-only" quotes look cheap until you learn what got left behind.</p>
<h3>Permits and Florida code</h3>
<p>Every AC replacement in Pasco, Hillsborough, and Polk county requires a mechanical permit and inspection. That is not optional and it is not a contractor upcharge invented to pad the bill. The permit protects you: it forces the 15.2 SEER2 minimum, proper electrical, a safety float switch on the drain pan, and code-compliant condensate handling. A quote that skips the permit to look cheaper is a quote that skips your protection.</p>
<h3>The matched AHRI system</h3>
<p>The SEER2 rating on the sticker only applies when the condenser, coil, and air handler are matched per the AHRI directory. A mismatched pairing can drop a 16 SEER2 rating to 14.5 SEER2 in the field, meaning you paid for efficiency you will never see. Always get the AHRI certificate number on your quote. Our <a href="/services/hvac-installation-tampa/">HVAC installation</a> quotes include it.</p>
<h3>Brand and tier</h3>
<p>Brand moves the price less than most homeowners expect. As we lay out in our <a href="/blogs/carrier-vs-trane-vs-rheem-tampa/">Carrier vs Trane vs Rheem comparison</a>, a mid-tier Rheem, Carrier, or Trane 3-ton install clusters in the $9,000 to $12,000 band, while premium variable-speed models from any of them push $14,000 to $18,000. We install Carrier, Trane, Lennox, Rheem, Goodman, American Standard, and Bryant, and as a Rheem Pro Partner we are transparent about that relationship.</p>

<h2>Full-system replacement versus condenser-only versus a compressor swap</h2>
<p>Not every failure requires a full changeout, and a good contractor will tell you which of three very different jobs you actually need.</p>
<h3>Full-system replacement</h3>
<p>This is condenser, evaporator coil, and air handler replaced together as a matched AHRI system. It is the right call when the system is 12-plus years old, when the coil and condenser use the phased-out R-410A refrigerant, or when the air handler is corroded. It costs the most up front but resets the clock on the whole system, restores full efficiency, and starts a fresh warranty. Every range in this guide's size and SEER2 tables refers to this full-system scope.</p>
<h3>Condenser-only replacement</h3>
<p>Replacing just the outdoor unit is cheaper, roughly $4,500 to $7,500 for a matched condenser and coil, but it is only appropriate when the indoor air handler is newer and can be matched to the new outdoor unit per AHRI. Pairing a brand-new condenser with an aged, mismatched indoor coil voids the efficiency rating and often the warranty. If someone quotes condenser-only on a 15-year-old system, ask hard questions.</p>
<h3>Just a compressor swap</h3>
<p>The compressor is the heart of the outdoor unit, and it can fail while the rest of the system is sound. If the unit is still under manufacturer parts warranty, a compressor replacement may cost you only labor. Out of warranty, the industry range for a compressor replacement runs <strong>$1,800 to $2,900</strong> (Forbes Home 2026, HomeAdvisor 2026), and here is the honest part: on an older system, spending $2,500 on a compressor to prop up a 13-year-old unit that will need a coil next summer is usually throwing good money after bad. That is where the repair-versus-replace math comes in.</p>

<h2>Repair versus replace: the $5,000 rule</h2>
<p>When a Tampa Bay homeowner calls us mid-July with a dead system, the real question is rarely "can you fix it" and almost always "should I fix it or replace it." Here is the rule of thumb the trade uses, and it works because it is simple: <strong>multiply the age of the system in years by the cost of the repair. If the result is over $5,000, replace. If it is under $5,000, repairing is usually reasonable.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A 6-year-old system with an $800 repair:</strong> 6 x 800 = 4,800. Under $5,000. Fix it.</li>
<li><strong>An 8-year-old system with a $700 fan motor:</strong> 8 x 700 = 5,600. Over $5,000. Lean replace, especially if it uses old refrigerant.</li>
<li><strong>A 12-year-old system with a $2,500 compressor:</strong> 12 x 2,500 = 30,000. Far over $5,000. Replace, no question.</li>
</ul>
<p>Layer three Florida-specific factors on top of the arithmetic. First, refrigerant: if your system runs R-410A, which was phased down starting in 2025, refrigerant for future repairs is getting scarcer and pricier, which tilts older systems toward replacement. Second, efficiency: a 13-year-old 12-SEER unit is costing you real money every month against a modern 15.2 SEER2 system, so a repair that keeps an energy hog alive has a hidden cost. Third, timing: systems die during peak Florida summer because that is when they work hardest, and a mid-August emergency repair on a dying unit often just delays the replacement by a few sweaty weeks. Same-day service is often available, but a band-aid on a failing 14-year-old system is not the same as a fix.</p>

<h2>Why matched sizing matters more than the sticker price</h2>
<p>Here is the counterintuitive truth after 16 years of Tampa Bay installs: the cheapest quote and the biggest system are both usually the wrong answer, and the reason is Manual J. A Manual J load calculation measures how much cooling your specific house actually needs based on square footage, orientation, windows, insulation, ceiling height, and Florida's brutal latent (humidity) load. Sizing a system to that number, rather than just matching the tonnage of whatever was there before, is what separates a system that keeps you comfortable from one that fights you for 15 years.</p>
<p>Oversizing is the classic Florida mistake. A system that is too big cools the air fast, hits the thermostat setpoint, and shuts off before it has run long enough to pull humidity out of the air. You end up with a house that is 74 degrees and clammy, that "muggy when the AC is off" feeling, plus short-cycling that wears out the compressor early. Undersizing leaves you unable to hold temperature on the worst August afternoons. Only a proper load calc gets it right, and a contractor who sizes by eyeballing your old unit is skipping the single most important step. This is exactly why the honest number can only come from an in-home assessment, not a phone quote.</p>

<h2>Red flags of a lowball quote</h2>
<p>A quote that comes in dramatically under the ranges in this guide is not a bargain; it is a warning. Something got cut, and it is usually something that will cost you later. Watch for these:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>No permit.</strong> Skipping the mechanical permit and inspection shaves a few hundred dollars and removes your legal protection, code enforcement, and often resale documentation. Never worth it.</li>
<li><strong>Condenser-only on an old system.</strong> A cheap price that swaps just the outdoor unit and leaves a mismatched, aging indoor coil kills your efficiency and often your warranty.</li>
<li><strong>No AHRI matched-system certificate.</strong> If they cannot give you the AHRI number, you have no proof the SEER2 rating you paid for is the rating you will get.</li>
<li><strong>No Manual J.</strong> "We will just match your old size" is the sound of a contractor skipping the load calc and gambling with your comfort.</li>
<li><strong>Used or gray-market equipment.</strong> Suspiciously low equipment cost sometimes means refurbished units or equipment sourced outside authorized channels, which can void the manufacturer warranty entirely.</li>
<li><strong>Vague, verbal-only pricing.</strong> A real quote itemizes equipment, labor, permit, line set handling, drain pan, and float switch in writing. A number scribbled on the back of a card is not a quote.</li>
</ol>
