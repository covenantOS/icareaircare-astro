<h2>Why your AC runs but won't cool the house</h2>
<p>When your air conditioner is running but not cooling, the blower is clearly alive, air is moving at the vents, and yet the house keeps climbing toward the outdoor temperature. That specific combination, a system that runs without lowering the temperature, actually narrows the problem to a short and predictable list. In sixteen years of Tampa Bay service calls, we have learned that the same eight root causes explain the overwhelming majority of "why is my ac not cooling" questions we get, and they fall into a clear order from most likely and cheapest to least likely and most expensive.</p>
<p>This is the umbrella guide. We walk each cause in that order, tell you exactly what you can safely check yourself versus what needs a licensed technician, and then show you how a pro confirms the real fault in about fifteen minutes. Where a single cause deserves its own deep dive, we link out rather than repeat it. If you would rather skip straight to a fix, Tim and the team handle <a href="/services/ac-repair-tampa/">AC repair across Tampa</a> most days on a same-day basis when the route schedule allows.</p>
<p>One Florida reality frames everything below. Tampa Bay logs more than 2,200 cooling hours a year, roughly double a northern climate, and your equipment lives in attic heat and 60 to 90 percent humidity for months at a stretch. Parts that might last fifteen years up north wear out faster here, and a small airflow problem that a milder climate would tolerate turns into a no-cool call in a Wesley Chapel July. Runtime is the enemy, and it is why our failure order below is tuned to Florida homes specifically.</p>

<h2>Safe DIY checks versus when to call a pro</h2>
<p>Before the ranked list, draw the line clearly. There are a handful of checks any homeowner can and should make first, because they are free, safe, and resolve a real share of no-cool calls without a truck ever rolling. Then there is everything that lives behind a sealed refrigerant circuit or a live 240-volt terminal, which is licensed-technician territory in Florida for good reason.</p>
<p><strong>Safe to check yourself:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Thermostat mode and setpoint, and fresh thermostat batteries.</li>
<li>The air filter, pulled and held up to the light.</li>
<li>Whether the outdoor unit is actually running when the indoor blower runs.</li>
<li>The breaker in your panel and the disconnect box on the wall by the condenser.</li>
<li>Ice on the indoor coil or the copper line set, which is a visual you can spot without opening anything.</li>
<li>Whether the condensate drain pan is full or the float switch has tripped.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Call a licensed tech for:</strong> anything involving refrigerant, capacitors, contactors, the compressor, or coil cleaning that requires disassembly. Refrigerant work legally requires <strong>EPA Section 608 certification</strong>, and a charged capacitor can hold a dangerous jolt long after the power is off. Our lead tech and owner, Tim Hawk, holds Florida license <strong>CAC1816515</strong> and the EPA Section 608 Universal certification, and a diagnostic visit runs a flat <strong>$89 that we credit toward any repair</strong> done on the same call.</p>

<h2>1. Thermostat and settings (start here, costs nothing)</h2>
<p>It sounds too simple, but a meaningful share of "home ac not cooling" calls end at the thermostat. Confirm the mode is set to COOL, not HEAT or FAN ONLY. Fan-only circulates room-temperature air all day and feels exactly like an air conditioner not blowing cold air. Confirm the setpoint is several degrees below the current room temperature so the system is actually being asked to cool. Replace the batteries if the screen is dim or blank, and check that nobody set a schedule or "hold" that is fighting you.</p>
<p>This is fully DIY. If COOL is selected, the setpoint is low, and the system still will not drop the temperature, you have ruled out the cheapest cause and can move down the list with confidence.</p>

<h2>2. Dirty air filter (5-minute check, biggest hidden cause)</h2>
<p>A clogged filter is the most common airflow problem we find on a no-cool call, and it is entirely preventable. When the filter is packed with dust and pet hair, the blower cannot pull enough air across the evaporator coil. Cooling output collapses, and in Florida the restricted airflow often freezes the coil solid, which we cover next. Pull the filter and hold it to a light. If you cannot see light through it, replace it.</p>
<p>Change filters every 30 to 60 days during our long cooling season, more often with pets or construction dust. This is DIY, and it is the single highest-value habit for keeping a system cooling. Persistent airflow trouble despite a clean filter can point to a dirty blower wheel or crushed return duct, which is where a technician earns the visit. Regular <a href="/services/ac-maintenance-tampa/">AC maintenance in Tampa</a> catches these before they strand you in July.</p>

<h2>3. Frozen evaporator coil (you can spot it, do not chip it)</h2>
<p>If your ac is not cooling but running, and you see frost or a block of ice on the indoor coil or on the copper refrigerant line near the air handler, the coil has frozen. A frozen coil is a symptom, not a root cause. It comes from restricted airflow (dirty filter or blower) or from low refrigerant, and while it is frozen the system blows warm or mildly cool air because ice is an insulator.</p>
<p>What you can safely do: turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON to help the ice melt, and replace a dirty filter. Never chip at the ice with anything, and never keep running a frozen system, because slugs of liquid refrigerant returning to the compressor can cause the most expensive failure on this list. Once thawed, if it freezes again the underlying cause is unresolved and needs a technician. We break down the freeze-and-thaw cycle further in our guide to <a href="/blogs/ac-blowing-warm-air-in-tampa-heres-how-to-fix-it-fast/">AC blowing warm air in Tampa</a>.</p>

<h2>4. Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse (electrical, mostly DIY)</h2>
<p>Here the pattern usually shifts: the indoor blower runs, but the outdoor condenser is dead silent. Walk to your electrical panel and look for a breaker that is tripped or sitting halfway. Reset it once, firmly to OFF then ON. Then check the pull-out disconnect box on the exterior wall beside the condenser, which sometimes gets pulled for lawn work and never seated back. Reseating it is safe DIY.</p>
<p>Inside that disconnect box, however, are cartridge fuses, and a blown fuse points to an electrical fault that should be diagnosed, not simply replaced blind. If a breaker trips again immediately after you reset it, stop. That is the system protecting itself from a short or a failing component, and repeatedly forcing it is dangerous. For how to read the difference, see our walkthrough on <a href="/blogs/how-to-tell-if-ac-fuse-is-blown/">how to tell if an AC fuse is blown</a>. When the electrical fault is real, our <a href="/services/ac-repair-tampa/">Tampa AC repair</a> team traces it to the source rather than papering over it.</p>

<h2>5. Failed capacitor or contactor (technician territory)</h2>
<p>This is the classic Florida summer failure. The outdoor unit hums for a second or two and then goes quiet, or the fan spins while the compressor stays silent, and the house slowly warms up. Nine times out of ten in our service area, that is a failed <strong>run capacitor</strong>, the small can that gives the compressor and fan their startup kick. Florida heat is brutal on capacitors, which live in a cabinet running well past 140 degrees for six months a year.</p>
<p>A failed run capacitor is typically a <strong>$180 to $320</strong> repair with parts and labor, and most calls resolve on the same visit. The <strong>contactor</strong>, the electrical switch that sends power to the compressor, pits and burns over thousands of Florida cycles and runs <strong>$150 to $350</strong> to replace. Both sit behind live high-voltage terminals and can store a charge, so this is a licensed-technician job, not a DIY one. For the warning signs of a dying contactor, read our detailed post on <a href="/blogs/ac-contactor-failure-signs/">AC contactor failure signs</a>. These two parts account for a large share of the no-cool calls we run, which is why they sit here in the middle of the list rather than at the bottom.</p>

<h2>6. Dirty condenser coil (part maintenance, part service)</h2>
<p>The outdoor unit rejects your home's heat through its condenser coil, the fins wrapping the outside of the cabinet. In Tampa Bay those fins get packed with grass clippings, dryer lint, cottonwood, and the fine sandy dust our yards throw off. When the coil is caked, the system cannot dump heat, head pressure climbs, and cooling output drops even though everything is technically running. You will often feel the air at the vents is only slightly cool rather than cold.</p>
<p>Homeowners can safely rinse the outdoor coil gently with a garden hose from the inside out, with the power off at the disconnect, and keep two feet of clearance around the cabinet. What you should not do is use a pressure washer, which bends the delicate fins, or open the cabinet. A proper coil cleaning with the correct chemistry and a fin comb is part of professional maintenance, and it restores cooling capacity that a caked coil has been quietly stealing all season.</p>

<h2>7. Low refrigerant or a leak (licensed only, never a DIY top-off)</h2>
<p>If the whole system is clean, airflow is good, and it still will not cool, low refrigerant moves up the suspect list. An AC does not consume refrigerant. If the charge is low, there is a leak, full stop. Symptoms include weak cooling that gets worse over weeks, a coil that keeps freezing, hissing or bubbling near the line set, and higher electric bills as the system runs nonstop trying to catch up. This is a leading reason an ac unit will not cool a house even though it runs constantly.</p>
<p>This one is strictly licensed-technician work. Handling refrigerant requires EPA Section 608 certification, and "just adding a can" without finding the leak is both illegal for uncertified hands and pointless, because the charge simply leaks back out. A correct repair means finding the leak, fixing it, evacuating the system, and weighing in the exact factory charge. If the leak is in the evaporator coil itself, replacement runs <strong>$1,400 to $2,600</strong>, at which point the age of the whole system factors into repair-versus-replace. Our licensed techs pressure-test and locate the leak rather than guessing.</p>

<h2>8. Failing compressor (least likely, most expensive)</h2>
<p>The compressor is the heart of the outdoor unit, and when it fails, cooling stops entirely even though the fan and blower may keep running. It is last on this list because it is genuinely the least common cause and, thankfully, often the result of something upstream that went unaddressed: a bad capacitor that made it strain, a refrigerant leak that starved it, or years of running frozen. That is exactly why fixing the cheaper causes early protects the expensive part.</p>
<p>Symptoms include the outdoor unit tripping the breaker the instant it tries to start, loud clanking or grinding, or a compressor that is hot and silent. There is nothing safe to DIY here. A technician confirms it with electrical testing before condemning a compressor, because a $250 capacitor is a far more common culprit than a five-figure compressor. When a compressor truly is gone on an older system, the honest conversation is usually about replacement, and we will lay out both paths with real numbers rather than pushing you one way.</p>

<h2>How a technician diagnoses it in about 15 minutes</h2>
<p>When our truck arrives, we are not guessing down this list one part at a time. A handful of measurements confirm the real fault fast, and here is what we are actually reading:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Temperature split:</strong> we measure the air temperature at a return grille and at a supply vent. A healthy Florida system pulls a 16 to 22 degree split. A split under about 14 degrees points to low charge, airflow restriction, or a coil problem, and immediately narrows the search.</li>
<li><strong>Static pressure:</strong> a manometer across the air handler tells us whether the blower is fighting a clogged filter, a dirty coil, or crushed ductwork before we ever touch the refrigerant side.</li>
<li><strong>Capacitor microfarads:</strong> a meter reads the capacitor against its nameplate rating. More than 10 percent below spec and it gets replaced, which resolves that classic humming-but-not-spinning outdoor unit on the spot.</li>
<li><strong>Superheat and subcooling:</strong> gauges on the refrigerant circuit reveal whether the charge is correct. This is how we confirm a leak and a low charge with numbers instead of a hunch, and why a refrigerant call is never a blind top-off.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The Florida factor: why these fail here first</h2>
<p>Everything above happens faster and harder in Tampa Bay than almost anywhere else in the country. Those 2,200-plus cooling hours mean your compressor, capacitor, and contactor cycle far more times per year than the same parts in a mild climate. Attic air handlers bake at 120-plus degrees, which cooks capacitors and stresses electrical connections. And our humidity means every system is fighting latent load, so a marginal airflow or charge problem that a dry climate would shrug off shows up here as a full no-cool call. That is also why a system running nonstop and still not cooling on a 95-degree afternoon is often a sign the equipment is simply undersized or overdue for service, not necessarily broken.</p>
<p>The practical takeaway: keep the filter fresh, keep the outdoor coil clear, and get a professional tune-up before each cooling season. Those three habits prevent most of the causes on this list from ever stranding you.</p>
