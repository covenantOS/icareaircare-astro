<h2>A dirty coil is a felted mat on wet aluminum</h2>

<p>The evaporator coil sits behind a panel in your air handler. In most Wesley Chapel and <a href="/service-areas/land-o-lakes-hvac-services/">Land O' Lakes</a> houses that cabinet lives in a garage, a closet, or a hot attic. Warm indoor air is supposed to wash across thin aluminum fins so the refrigerant inside the tubes can pick up heat. Dust, pet hair, and construction grit that sneak around the filter rack land on those fins. Florida coils stay wet for months, so the dust does not sit as a dry film. It turns into a gray felt that plugs the gaps between fins.</p>

<p>That mat does two jobs you do not want. It blocks airflow the same way a packed filter does. It also insulates the metal from the air it is supposed to cool. Supply vents get weaker. The house takes longer to drop a degree. The coil surface falls closer to freezing, and then you are on our <a href="/blogs/frozen-evaporator-coil-tampa/">frozen evaporator coil</a> page instead of this one.</p>

<blockquote><strong>Tim's tip:</strong> if you changed the filter last week and the vents still feel lazy, do not buy a higher MERV filter as the next move. A tight filter on a dirty coil just starves the blower more. Pull the filter, hold it to a light, and then call. The panel behind it is where we look first.</blockquote>

<h2>Symptoms you can check without pulling the coil panel</h2>

<p>You usually cannot see the coil without screws and a flashlight. You can still diagnose a dirty coil from the rooms you actually live in.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Weak airflow on a new filter.</strong> A clogged filter is the cheaper twin of this problem. If the filter is clean and the registers still whisper, the restriction moved downstream onto the coil.</li>
<li><strong>Long runtimes with a small temperature drop.</strong> The outdoor unit runs, the indoor blower runs, and the thermostat barely moves. A healthy split across the coil in this climate is roughly 18 to 22 degrees. A dirty coil often lands well below that.</li>
<li><strong>Ice on the big insulated copper line after you already changed the filter.</strong> Airflow freeze and refrigerant freeze look similar from the driveway. A freeze that comes back on a clean filter is coil or charge, not the cardboard rectangle in the slot.</li>
<li><strong>A house that feels clammy at setpoint.</strong> The coil has to stay wet and cold long enough to wring humidity. A blanketed coil spends more of its time fighting airflow and less of its time dehumidifying.</li>
<li><strong>Musty air at the start of a cycle.</strong> Biofilm grows on a wet, dusty coil. That smell is not a perfume problem. It is biology on aluminum.</li>
</ul>

<p>Ice on the outdoor unit in August is a different fault. The condenser should be hot. Frost out there belongs to a technician with gauges, not a garden hose.</p>

<h2>Why Tampa Bay coils foul faster than the brochure says</h2>

<p>National maintenance pages talk about an annual coil cleaning like it is a calendar courtesy. Systems here log more than 2,200 cooling hours a year. Filters in homes with pets or a nearby build load in 30 to 60 days. Fine dust still bypasses a 1-inch filter rack, especially on older return drops in unincorporated Lutz and east Pasco.</p>

<p>Attic air handlers bake. The blower works harder against high static pressure from flex duct that got crushed during storage. More hours plus more moisture plus more dust is the felted mat. New construction in Epperson, Mirada, and Wiregrass adds drywall dust for the first year even when the builder left a filter in. If you just closed on a house, treat the first cooling season as a coil-inspection season, not a set-and-forget season.</p>

<p>Outdoor pollen on the condenser is a separate layer. Rinse the outdoor coil with the disconnect pulled and a garden hose. Do not confuse that rinse with an indoor evaporator cleaning. Different chemistry, different access, different risk to the fins.</p>

<h2>Cleaning versus replacement, and who should do which</h2>

<p>A light coating comes off with the right no-rinse or rinse coil cleaner, a fin comb, and a drain flush so the dirt you just loosened does not clog the trap. That is a maintenance or repair visit, not a Saturday project with dish soap. Household cleaners etch aluminum. A pressure washer folds fins shut. Both turn a cleanable coil into a restricted coil you then have to replace.</p>

<p>Replacement enters the conversation when the fins are packed so hard that cleaner will not restore airflow, when the coil is leaking, or when the system is old enough that the coil job is half a changeout. Published national ranges for evaporator coil replacement sit around <strong>$600 to $2,000</strong>, with a commonly cited average near <strong>$1,350</strong> (<a href="https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/heating-and-cooling/ac-evaporator-coil-replacement" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">HomeAdvisor</a>; <a href="https://www.thisoldhouse.com/heating-cooling/evaporator-coil-replacement-cost" target="_blank" rel="noopener nofollow">This Old House</a> gives $627 to $2,700 with the same average). Those are market figures, not an I Care Air Care quote. Tonnage, access, refrigerant type, and whether the coil is still under manufacturer parts warranty move the number a lot. We are a Rheem Pro Partner and we look up the serial before we talk replacement.</p>

<p>A cleaning that does not hold, with frost back on the suction line within a week, is not a dirty-coil problem anymore. That pattern is a leak. Read the companion post on <a href="/blogs/evaporator-coil-leak-tampa/">evaporator coil leaks</a> before you pay for a second wash.</p>
